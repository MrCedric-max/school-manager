# Why the book is shaped this way — CBA sources

The Class 1 Integrated Scheme of Work fixes *what* is taught each week. It does not fix
the *shape* of a pupil's page. This note records what that shape was based on, so the
choices can be defended or changed.

## 1. The approach itself: CBA / APC-ESV

- Cameroon's primary curriculum (MINEDUB, *Cameroon Primary School Curriculum, English
  Subsystem, Level I: Class 1 & 2*, 2018) moves from the objective-based approach to the
  **Competency-Based Approach**, and names **Project Based Learning**, supported by
  Integrated Theme Learning and Cooperative Learning, as the major teaching-learning
  approach. The scheme of work we follow is already organised that way: themes, weekly
  competencies, a culminating project per theme.
- In the francophone subsystem the same reform is called **APC-ESV** — *approche par
  compétences avec entrée par les situations de vie*: learning is entered through
  situations of life, not through lists of content.
- The literature (De Ketele, Roegiers; and the Cameroonian syllabus analyses) describes a
  competence as the ability to **mobilise an integrated set of resources to solve a
  problem belonging to a family of situations**. Three consequences for a textbook:
  1. start from a situation, not from a word list;
  2. practise the resources (words, sounds, structures) separately, then
  3. **re-integrate** them in a new situation — otherwise the learner can recite but not act.

That is why every unit has **Let's start** (entry situation), the domain steps (resources),
and **I integrate** (a fresh situation using the week's language). The "I integrate" step
is the one most primary textbooks skip.

## 2. The seven domains

The scheme lists, for every theme: Listening and Speaking; Reading; Writing; Grammar,
Vocabulary and Pronunciation; Phonics; Literature and Drama; Culminating Project. The
book keeps that order and labels every step with its domain, so a teacher can see the
scheme in the page, and an inspector can check coverage at a glance.

## 3. Assessment: criteria, not marks

CBA assessment is **criterion-referenced**: the learner is judged against criteria, at
levels, and is expected to reach mastery rather than be ranked. Hence:

- three criteria per week, written in the first person ("I can…");
- three levels: **Not yet / Getting there / Yes, I can** — no percentage, no zero;
- the same criteria on the project page, so pupil and teacher tick together;
- an explicit **remediation** instruction: if you are at "Not yet", you practise again
  rather than move on.

Self-assessment boxes are not decoration: in CBA the learner is meant to know what
"good" looks like. The front matter explains the three levels to the child.

## 4. Project-based learning: the strips

Each theme's culminating project in the scheme is a *strip* — a folded card with four
parts. The book makes the strip the spine of the theme: week 1 adds one thing, week 2 the
next, and the fourth week is the presentation. That is project-based learning in the
smallest viable form for a 5–6 year old: one artefact, four small steps, one public
performance, revisited every month.

## 5. What was deliberately *not* copied from commercial textbooks

- No dense reading passages: Class 1 pupils in Cameroon are decoding monosyllables.
- No long vocabulary lists: 4–5 new words a week, 127 in the year, all from the scheme.
- No instructions addressed to the teacher inside the pupil's page, except the small
  italic "how to do it" line — those belong in the teacher's guide.
